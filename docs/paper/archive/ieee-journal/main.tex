\begin{abstract}
We present and evaluate a system that lets a person control a laptop without touching it. The user looks at a calibrated screen location, pinches to grab a virtual volume knob, turns the wrist to change the volume and pinches again to release. Head orientation comes from consumer Sony headphones. Wrist motion and raw photoplethysmography (PPG) come from a Samsung Galaxy Watch over Bluetooth Low Energy, and all inference runs on the laptop behind a fail-closed safety chain. Because the watch cannot see the fingers, we recorded wrist signals with camera hand landmarks as reference timing annotations. We evaluated detectors as continuous event detectors, counting caught pinches against false activations per hour of no-gesture time. Wrist PPG alone generalised poorly to a new day (AUC 0.60--0.64), and six robustness methods did not change this. Fusing the inertial sensors raised worst-split AUC from 0.789 to 0.837, and firing at the score's peak added 4--12 recall points. A three-network 1-D convolutional model on nine raw channels outperformed tree ensembles, but held-out days varied widely (43\% of 372 starts pooled; about two thirds on the newer days). A later model trained on hand-corrected labels caught 47 of 57 pinch-in starts with no false activations over 0.48~h on its development evaluation set (median latency 442~ms), a result we reproduced by replaying the stored model. Median-based normalisation removed a motion-induced blind spot. We report the system design, data pipeline and results, and analyse the evaluation and labelling practices that determined whether offline results predicted live behaviour.
\end{abstract}
